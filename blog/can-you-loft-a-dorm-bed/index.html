Can You Loft a Dorm Bed? Heights, Rules & Safety Guide

By DormToolJuly 15, 2026 · 7 min readDorm Setup

A friend's roommate posted a photo in the group chat recently: their bed frame raised way up, with a neat row of storage bins lined up underneath — clean enough to look like a magazine spread. Suddenly that patch of floor a bed normally eats up was an entire extra layer of usable space. The first thought was: wait, you can just do that? Can I do that with my bed?

Then the questions start piling up. How high can you actually go? Do you do it yourself, or does the school have to do it? Is it even safe?

Here's the short answer: there's no single answer — it depends entirely on your specific school's policy. Some schools have self-adjustable beds you can raise yourself with a rubber mallet. Some require maintenance staff to do it for you. And a few don't allow any adjustment at all, not even bed risers. This guide walks through what to check, the height ranges you'll typically see, the safety rules that matter, and what's worth buying once you've got the extra space.

What Does "Lofting a Bed" Actually Mean?

Freshmen mix this up with bunking all the time — they're not the same thing.

Raised Height (aka Captain Height or Semi-Lofted)

The bed frame set to the highest of its "low" settings — usually around 28–30 inches off the floor, with just enough clearance underneath for the dresser your school provides. This is typically the top setting most schools let you use without any extra request or approval.

Fully Lofted

The bed frame raised significantly higher, with clearance underneath of roughly 50–70 inches — enough to stand under, and enough room for a small couch, a mini fridge, or even a desk. This level usually needs extra hardware, a guard rail, and sometimes a formal request through your housing portal.

Bunked

Two separate beds stacked on top of each other, usually set up in triple or quad rooms to save floor space. This is a different configuration from lofting a single bed — some rooms are bunked by default, with no option to loft instead.

Knowing which of these you're actually asking about makes the conversation with your school a lot more specific — and a lot faster.

Can You Loft Your Dorm Bed? (It Depends on Your School)

Three situations come up most often.

1. Self-adjustable beds you raise yourself

Most dorm bed frames have several notches or slots. You (or the front desk during move-in) use a rubber mallet to knock the horizontal rails out of one slot and into a higher one. Straightforward, and usually the fastest option if your school allows it.

2. Lofting available, but only through maintenance

Some schools require you to submit a work order through the housing portal ahead of time, often with a cutoff date (commonly a few weeks before move-in). Requests get backed up during peak season, so the earlier you submit, the better your odds of getting it done before you arrive.

3. Not allowed to adjust at all

A handful of schools lock the bed height entirely for fire code or safety reasons — this can include a ban on bed risers too. Don't try to work around this on your own; getting caught can mean a maintenance charge or a conduct issue.
This one detail won't show up in any generic packing list — it only lives on your own school's housing portal. Before you buy anything for under-bed storage, spend five minutes searching "bed height" or "lofting policy" on your housing site to confirm which of the three situations applies to you.

How High Can You Loft a Dorm Bed?

Rough reference ranges — always confirm against your own school's numbers.

Standard (factory) height

About 20 inches off the floor. This is what most beds ship at before any adjustment.

Raised / Captain Height

Roughly 28–30 inches, with about 20–22 inches of clearance underneath — enough for a dresser, not much more.

Fully Lofted

Clearance underneath can reach 50–70 inches, enough to stand under and fit a couch, mini fridge, or small desk. If your bed frame is self-adjustable, it typically has 5–9 notches — move up one at a time until you hit the height that works for your setup.

Do You Need a Safety Rail?

Short answer: yes, if your bed is raised or bunked.

Rail height matters

A guard rail is strongly recommended — and required by most schools — any time your bed is at raised or bunked height. The rail should sit at least 5 inches above the top of your mattress. For example, a 6-inch mattress plus a 3-inch topper means your rail needs to be at least 14 inches tall. Taller is generally safer.

Don't skip it

If your school doesn't provide a rail by default, request one — or buy or rent your own. This isn't a step worth skipping to save a little hassle. Falls from a raised bed are a real risk, and it's worth taking seriously.

What to Buy If You're Lofting Your Bed

Once your bed is up, the floor space underneath becomes the most valuable square footage in your room.

Under-bed storage bins

Low-profile bins (5–6 inches tall) work well at raised height. If you're fully lofted, you can go bigger — larger bins or even a small dresser.

Mini fridge or microwave

If your clearance is tall enough, tucking these underneath frees up space next to your desk instead.

Small couch or floor cushion

A common move at full loft height — turning the space underneath into a second hangout spot.

Foldable desk or chair

With enough clearance, some students move their whole desk setup underneath and use it as a daytime study nook.

FAQ

Can I bring my own loft kit to college?

Most schools don't allow non-official lofting parts, due to fire code and structural safety rules. Confirm with your housing portal before you buy or pack anything — don't find out it's against policy after you've already brought it to campus.

Can I use bed risers if my school doesn't offer bed lofting?

It depends on the school. Some allow bed risers as a compromise to gain a few extra inches, while others ban them entirely over safety concerns. Check your specific school's policy rather than assuming — rules vary a lot between campuses.

What's the difference between a lofted bed and a bunked bed?

A lofted bed is a single bed raised up to free space underneath it. A bunked bed is two separate beds stacked on top of each other for two people. Some triple or quad rooms are set up as bunked by default, with no option to loft instead.

Do I need to lower my bed back down before I move out?

Most schools require you to return your bed to its default height before your move-out inspection, or you may be charged a fee. This is an easy detail to forget — add it to your move-out checklist well before the end of the semester.
