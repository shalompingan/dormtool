College Rent Affordability Calculator

How much rent can you really afford? Enter your income, debts, and expenses — we'll crunch the numbers using 3 different methods.

Calculate your rent budget to get started!

How to Use This Rent Affordability Calculator to Find Your Budget

Knowing how much rent you can afford is the first step to finding an apartment you can actually live in — without financial stress. This calculator uses three proven methods to give you a realistic rent budget based on your actual income and expenses.

Who Should Use This Rent Affordability Calculator

This tool is built for college students moving off campus, recent graduates renting their first apartment, and anyone who wants to make sure they're not overspending on rent. If you have student loans, a part-time job, or irregular income, this calculator adjusts for your real financial situation.

How to Use the Rent Affordability Calculator

Enter your monthly after-tax income, your monthly debt payments (student loans, car payments, credit cards), and your estimated utility costs. The calculator runs four methods — the 30% Rule, the 50-30-20 Budget, the Debt-to-Income (DTI) Method, and your Custom percentage — then recommends the safest (lowest) option as your max rent.

Understanding the Four Calculation Methods

The 30% Rule says spend no more than 30% of your income on rent. The 50-30-20 Method allocates 50% of income to needs (rent is 60% of that). The DTI Method subtracts debts from 36% of income, then subtracts utilities. The Custom % lets you set your own target. The calculator always recommends the most conservative number — the one that leaves you the most financial breathing room.

Tips for Staying Within Your Rent Budget

Remember that rent is just one part of your housing costs. Utilities, internet, parking, and renters insurance can add $150–$300 per month. Use the City Guide tab to compare rents in your target city, and the What-If tab to see how changing your income or debt affects your budget. If your target rent exceeds your safe budget, consider a studio, a roommate, or a less expensive neighborhood.

Rent Affordability Calculator — Frequently Asked Questions

How much rent can I afford with student loans?

Student loan payments reduce your available housing budget dollar for dollar. Enter your monthly loan payment in the Debt field, and the calculator will automatically adjust your max rent. Many students with loan payments find they can afford $200–$400 less per month than peers without debt.

Should I use the 30% rule or the DTI method?

Both are useful, but the DTI (Debt-to-Income) method is more accurate if you have student loans or other monthly debts. The 30% rule doesn't account for existing obligations. The calculator shows all four methods and recommends the most conservative (lowest) number.

What counts as utilities in this calculator?

Utilities include electricity, water, gas, internet, and any mandatory building fees (like trash or maintenance). Most students budget $100–$200 per month for these. If your apartment includes some utilities in the rent, adjust the utilities field down accordingly.

Can I afford an apartment if I have irregular income?

Yes, but use a conservative monthly average. Add up your total income from the last 3–6 months and divide by the number of months. If you work tips or freelance, consider using 80% of your average to build in a safety buffer for slower months.

What's the difference between gross and net income for rent calculations?

This calculator uses net (after-tax) income, which is more accurate for budgeting. Your gross income might be 20–30% higher, but using it would overestimate what you can actually afford. If you only know your gross income, subtract roughly 20–30% for taxes before entering it.

How does finding a roommate affect my rent budget?

With a roommate, you can split a 2-bedroom apartment and often each pay less than a studio. For example, a $1,800 2BR split two ways is $900 per person — often cheaper than a $1,200 studio. Use the What-If tab to adjust your income upward by the amount a roommate saves you.
